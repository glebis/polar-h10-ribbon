# LinkedIn Post: Teaching as Medicine

---

I wore a heart rate monitor for 48 hours straight. Not a wrist sensor — a medical-grade chest strap that measured 71,000 individual heartbeats.

I built a real-time biofeedback system with Claude Code that turns HRV data into ambient lighting, generative music, and clinical analytics. Then I analyzed what my nervous system actually does across different activities.

The data surprised me.

During a 90-minute teaching session at my Claude Code Lab, my heart rate variability hit 69ms — 2.3× my resting baseline. My heart rate dropped to 59 bpm, the lowest sustained rate ever recorded. I was speaking 92% of the time.

Teaching beat every deliberate health practice I tried:

♥ Teaching (flow state): 65ms
♥ Exercise + breathing: 44ms  
♥ Cold exposure: 40ms
♥ Coherence breathing: 38ms
♥ Resting baseline: 29ms

I've been researching HRV biofeedback as an anxiety management protocol — breathing exercises, cold showers, Zone 2 training. All evidence-based. All helpful. None came close to what happened when I was teaching.

The insight: anxiety doesn't live in the nervous system. It lives in the gaps between purpose.

When I'm idle, my nervous system spins into sympathetic dominance — fight-or-flight without a fight. When I'm deeply engaged in teaching, the default mode network quiets, the vagal brake engages, and my heart finds its rhythm.

The nervous system isn't broken. It's waiting for purpose.

This aligns with Csikszentmihalyi's flow research and Peifer (2012), who found that flow correlates with increased vagal tone and decreased cortisol. Porges' polyvagal theory explains the mechanism: teaching activates the ventral vagal circuit — the social engagement system. Voice, eye contact, reading the room — all vagal.

I'm not saying teaching cures anxiety. I'm saying that for me, the data shows meaningful engagement is a more powerful parasympathetic activator than any breathing exercise I've tried.

What puts you in flow? That might be your medicine too.
